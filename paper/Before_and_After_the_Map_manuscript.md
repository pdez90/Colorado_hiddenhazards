# Before and After the Map: Industrial Siting and the Making of Environmental Inequality in Denver, 1880–2020

Oct 18, 2026 · @Priyanka

*Draft for* Economic Geography. *Reproducible from the project repository (github.com/pdez90/Colorado\_hiddenhazards) and explorable at the Colorado Hidden Hazards application.*

**Abstract.** Environmental inequality is usually measured where regulatory inventories begin, in the 1980s, long after the industrial geography that produces it was laid down. This paper reconstructs that geography for Denver from its first state business directory (1871) to the present and asks what came first: hazardous industry, the people who lived beside it, or the instruments — the 1925 zoning ordinance and the 1938 HOLC map — that are often credited with creating the pattern. Three sources are linked: the street-level hazardous trades of 34 business-directory editions (1871–1956) and 13 complete manufacturers' directory editions (1965–1992); the complete-count censuses of 1880–1940 aggregated to reconstructed enumeration districts and census tracts within 10 km of downtown for 1950–2020; and the city's 1929 zoning map, the 1938 HOLC map and today's assessor parcels. Four findings follow. First, the disparities preceded both instruments. From 1880 to 1940 districts nearer hazardous industry held more foreign-born, labouring and lodging households and fewer owners, and the hazardous trades that opened before 1940 opened in places that already had more of those households. Second, the land the appraisers graded D combined two different geographies. Black Denver became concentrated in two residential D areas northeast of downtown between 1900 and 1930, before the map, while the industrially zoned land, in D and in the ungraded industrial zones, lost Black residents and became Mexican and Hispano. The D–C Black gap describes the first geography, not the second. Third, neither instrument coincides with an acceleration that the data can detect. Per directory edition, hazardous siting after 1938 favoured D land within the range it had before (D/C 1.9, against 1.7–6.2), and after 1950 the Hispanic concentration beside industry is strong between places but does not track a tract's own changes in industry. Hazardous manufacturers that arrived after 1965 went to tracts that were no poorer, and more often owner-occupied, than those that never had one, and those tracts then gained Hispanic residents much faster. Concentration of both groups in D and industrial tracts peaked around 1960 and has declined since. Fourth, half of the 1965–92 sites are still industrial parcels, and most of the 184 now residential were small firms in residential buildings rather than conversions. The instruments recorded an industrial and racial geography that already existed; the data do not show that either created it or measurably accelerated it.

**Keywords:** environmental justice; industrial geography; redlining; zoning; historical GIS; Denver

## 1. Introduction

Does hazardous industry find the poor, or do the poor find hazardous industry? The question has organised the environmental-justice literature since Been's (1994) challenge to the siting studies of the 1980s, and it has rarely been answered with evidence from the period when the answer was decided. Most studies measure exposure from inventories that begin in the 1980s — the Toxics Release Inventory, Superfund, RCRA — and composition from censuses after 1970, which places the observation window a century after the first smelters and tanneries were built in American cities and decades after the housing market had sorted people around them (Pastor, Sadd and Hipp 2001; Mohai and Saha 2015). Frickel and Elliott (2018) showed how much that window misses: in Portland, Philadelphia, Minneapolis and New Orleans the great majority of historical hazardous sites had closed before any inventory existed, most had been converted to other uses, and the inventories captured a small, recent and unrepresentative residue of the industrial land that still underlies the city. The historical origins of environmental inequality are, on this account, both the explanation of the present pattern and the part of it least often measured.

This paper reconstructs those origins for one city over 140 years. Denver is a useful case because it is a late, planned and well-documented city: it had a state business directory listing every trade from 1871, a manufacturers' directory from 1961 (with printed industry codes from 1965), one of the earliest comprehensive zoning ordinances in the mountain West (1925), a HOLC residential security map (1938), and a public library that holds the city directories, the zone maps and the Sanborn fire-insurance atlases that let the inventory be placed on the historical street grid. It is also a city whose environmental-justice geography is well known in its present form — the Globeville, Elyria-Swansea and Sun Valley neighbourhoods along the South Platte and the rail yards are the usual exhibits — so that the question of how that geography formed can be asked against a known end-point.

The argument runs in three steps, each with its own section of evidence. Section 3 uses the complete-count censuses of 1880–1940, aggregated to reconstructed enumeration districts, to describe who lived beside the hazardous trades in each decade before the instruments of the 1920s and 1930s existed. Section 4 brings in the 1925 zoning ordinance and the 1938 HOLC map and asks, in the design of Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder (2021), whether the map created the pattern or recorded it, using the map's own boundaries as the comparison and controlling for rail and for what the city had already zoned. Section 4 ends with descriptive sequences on fixed geographies — which came first in each place, industry or its neighbours, and whether anything changed when the zoning or the map arrived. Section 5 carries the analysis onto census tracts from 1950 to 2020, where the group beside industry changes. Section 6 asks what became of the sites themselves, matching every one to the parcel that stands there today, and Section 7 asks whether they are associated with mortality: among residents today, before and after the sites were rebuilt, and among Denver residents of 1940 followed to their deaths. Section 8 draws the steps together and sets out what the evidence can and cannot support.

The contribution is partly substantive and partly methodological. Substantively, the paper shows that a class and nativity gradient around hazardous industry in Denver predates zoning, covenants and redlining; that each of those instruments marked the same corridor; that the group most concentrated beside it after 1950 was Hispanic; and that the corridor persists in the parcel record. Methodologically, it demonstrates a reproducible chain — OCR of directory scans, geocoding to historical streets, district reconstruction from full-count microdata, texture classification of a hatched zoning map, and parcel matching — that can be run for any city with a directory series and a Sanborn atlas, and it sets out what such a chain needs in order not to overclaim: one definition of hazardous industry throughout, one study area, fixed-geography checks on every trend, and comparisons of firm density within one source rather than across a change of source. The code and the derived data are published (microdata and address files are not redistributed), and a companion web application shows every site, district, grade and parcel behind the tables.

## 2. Data and methods

**Hazardous industry, 1871–1992.** The inventory follows Frickel and Elliott (2018) in using city and state directories rather than regulatory lists as the primary record of where hazardous manufacturing stood, and uses their set of hazardous sectors throughout: the same sector definition selects the business-directory trades, the manufacturers and the firms counted in every model and map. Two series are used. The *Colorado State Business Directory* (1871–1956, 34 editions scanned by the Denver Public Library) lists every business by town and trade; the hazardous trades — smelting and refining, foundries and metal working, chemicals and paints, petroleum, tanneries, rendering, gas works, printing and plating — were extracted by OCR and keyword matching, and those with a street address (395 in Denver; the rest are placed at the town centre and excluded from every spatial analysis) were geocoded against the historical street grid. The street-level subset is the weakest part of these data: the text layers of the 1910–1956 volumes interleave two columns, so an entry often runs into its neighbour's, and in a spot check about one Denver street-level placement in five carried the neighbouring listing's address or name. Street-level pre-1960 counts are therefore indicative; the town-level counts are sound. The *Directory of Colorado Manufacturers* (Business Research Division, University of Colorado, 1961–2002) lists every manufacturer with its address and SIC code. Only the thirteen editions whose scans are complete (1965–68, 1970, 1973–74, 1977, 1980, 1983, 1988, 1990, 1992) are used anywhere in the paper; they yield 3,487 Colorado sites in the hazardous sectors after linking listings across editions within 30 m, 1,112 of them in the City and County of Denver. Twelve further editions survive only in part and are excluded. In an earlier build, thirty-four listings, 25 of them in the 1965 edition, had been placed by the geocoder's direction-loose pass on the unprefixed north-Denver street of a south-Denver address ("4000 S. Kalamath" on north Kalamath Street, in the industrial corridor). The geocoder now never places a prefixed address on the unprefixed street across the Ellsworth and Broadway baselines, and a final check, which sets to unlocated any Denver listing whose point lies on the wrong side of those baselines for its own direction, finds none. "Firms of the decade" for a census year are the listings of the ten years ending at it. Business-directory editions are uneven (none survives for 1893–1909, and only 1910 for the decade ending in 1910), so the number of firms of the decade before 1940 reflects which editions exist as much as how many firms there were, and counts of firms are not compared across censuses; for 2000–2020 the last complete decade, 1983–92, is carried forward, so those years measure proximity to historically listed sites, not to active manufacturing.

**Benchmarks.** The directories were compared with the public establishment counts. Against County Business Patterns (CBP), the complete editions list 0.69 (1992) to 1.20 (1980) as many Colorado hazardous-sector establishments as CBP counts (1977 1.12, 1983 1.14, 1988 0.98, 1990 1.00); the partial editions list 0.46–0.89. Across counties, the log directory count and the log CBP count correlate at 0.84 for all manufacturing and 0.88 for the hazardous sectors (15 editions, 63 counties; Denver's ratio is 0.94). CBP counts every employer establishment and has no addresses, so the ratios are coverage rates and CBP cannot replace the directory for siting. For 1880–1940 the business directory's hazardous trades are compared with the census of manufactures' county establishment counts (NHGIS), for the censuses with a directory edition within two years (1880, 1890, 1920–1940; 1900 has no directory edition within two years, since none survives between 1892 and 1910, and 1910 has no county manufacturing table), with each firm assigned to the county in force at that census; because the directory lists only hazardous trades, the ratio is a floor on its coverage.

**Archival check.** For twenty street-located trades listed in 1891–92 with exact addresses, the 1890 Sanborn sheets (Library of Congress) were read lot by lot. On five the sheet shows the trade at the geocoded lot (foundries on Blake and Delgany, the Colorado Lead Works, a tin shop on Stout); on five it shows a related use on the lot or immediately adjoining; on six it shows something else (a laundry, offices, a stable, a warehouse, a vacant lot); four are inconclusive (excavations or a lumber yard). Ten of the sixteen conclusive cases therefore place industrial work at or beside the geocode. The mismatches are what one would expect from directory addresses that name an office or a residence, and from a sheet surveyed a year or two before the listing; they are a reason to read the street-level results as neighbourhood-scale rather than lot-scale.

**Population, 1880–1940.** The complete-count censuses (Ruggles et al., IPUMS USA) give every person's enumeration district (ED) but no address, and no public source maps the EDs of 1900–1940. They were reconstructed by placing the addresses that Brown University's Geographic Reference Files give for every 1900–1940 household on the historical street grid and tessellating the city so that each ED's polygon contains its matched households (72% of the 1900 addresses are geocoded at street level, and the addresses kept after stray geocodes are dropped hold 60% of the 1900 population; 73% in 1940); 1880 uses the Urban Transition Historical GIS of Brown University, which geocoded that census to buildings, rebuilt the same way so that all six censuses are constructed alike. The EDs are those of Denver itself (Arapahoe County before 1902, the City and County of Denver after), and the mapped populations reproduce the county totals (for example 317,663 of 322,780 in 1940). The resulting districts (17 in 1880, 128–330 in 1900–1940) carry race, Hispanic origin, nativity, occupation (laborers; Duncan's SEI), tenure, rent and household structure. A measure is left missing in a census that did not ask the question rather than set to zero (ownership in 1880; SEI in the 1900 extract; rent and residence five years earlier only in 1940). Hispanic origin before 1980 is the IPUMS HISPAN variable, which IPUMS infers from birthplace, parents' birthplace and Spanish surname; it is not a self-identification and is not the same measure as the tract Hispanic counts of 1960 onwards, so the two series are compared in direction, not level. "Boarders and lodgers" are household members unrelated to the head (IPUMS RELATE 12–13). Only aggregates leave the project.

**Population, 1950–2020.** The study area for every tract analysis is one fixed set: the 147 census tracts (2010 boundaries) whose centroid lies within 10 km of downtown Denver. For 1990–2020 population, race and Hispanic origin come from the NHGIS standardised time series, already on 2010 tracts. Every other census and measure is moved from that census's own tracts to 2010 tracts by area weighting: counts are interpolated first and shares computed afterwards, with each share's denominator (persons, households, persons for whom poverty status is determined, and so on) interpolated from the same table as its numerator; medians are averaged with weights of occupied units times overlap area. Dollar measures are in 2020 prices; rent is gross rent from 1960 and contract rent for 1940–50. Tracts with fewer than 200 residents are dropped from every measure. Hispanic origin is first reported on tracts in 1960 (Spanish surname or language), so the tract panel has no Hispanic measure for 1940–50; the 1940 HOLC tract value used in Section 4 alone is filled from the 1940 districts and is labelled so. The 1940 tract tables report nonwhite rather than Black, and the 1940 tract "Black" figures in Section 4 are nonwhite shares.

Not every one of the 147 tracts was tracted in every census: 97 had a population of 200 or more on 1950 tracts, 144 in 1960 and 145–147 from 1970. A trend computed over "all tracts" in each year therefore compares partly different places, and every tract result is repeated on the fixed sets of tracts observed from 1950 (95 tracts), 1960 (142) and 1970 (144).

**Instruments.** The 1938 HOLC residential security map and area descriptions are from Mapping Inequality (Nelson, Winling et al.); 730 racially restrictive deeds of 1931–35 from the Denver Public Library's Mapping Prejudice pilot. The 1925 zoning ordinance is taken from the official *Building Zone Map* as revised to January 1929 (Denver Public Library, CG4314 .D4 G44 1929), a monochrome hatched map: a texture classifier labelled 32-pixel cells of the scan as Industrial A, Industrial B or Commercial by hatch orientation and density, and the result was georeferenced from 15 control points (street intersections and park corners) resolved to TIGER road intersections (affine fit in UTM 13N, RMSE 19 m). Residence E and Industrial A share a hatch and are separated only by the absence of lot lines, the classification's main uncertainty. The map sheet does not cover the whole of the 1938 graded area; zoning shares are computed only for land on the sheet, and units less than half on it are left missing. The library's other edition, the same map revised to April 1944, was registered to the 1929 scan by a ten-point affine fit and compared with it cell by cell. One texture classifier trained on the 1929 labels was applied to both scans; every cluster of disagreement of about a city block or more (118 of 226 clusters) was inspected on side-by-side crops of the two scans, and only districts visibly redrawn were accepted (8 areas). Most disagreements came from the darker 1944 print rather than from redrawn districts; changes smaller than a block, and amendments coloured by hand on the 1944 copy, are not included. The next citywide map is the Zone Map, City and County of Denver of 1 July 1955, prepared by the Department of Zoning Administration for the comprehensive ordinance that replaced the 1925 one (Ordinance No. 16, Series of 1955, itself replaced in 1956 by Ordinance No. 392; History Colorado, rG4314 .D4G44 1955 .D4). Its fifteen districts are printed as black-and-white patterns; a texture classifier trained on hand-drawn boxes of known pattern labelled 46 m cells of the scan, and only the industrial districts are used (I-1, vertical dashes, and I-2, rings of dots; the map shows no I-0 land), since cells predicted as business on the edges of the narrow business strips were often residential. Of 60 random cells near industrial land, judged by eye on the scan, 58 were classified correctly. The map was georeferenced from 23 street intersections by a second-order polynomial (RMSE 13 m, leave-one-out 17 m). Today's land use is the City and County of Denver assessor parcel layer (property class, current zone district, dwelling units, year built), matched to the parcel within 40 m of each site whose situs address matches, else the nearest (median 4 m). Regulatory status is from EPA FRS, TRI, SEMS, ACRES brownfields and RCRA (handlers and corrective action) and Colorado's cleanup, Superfund, brownfield, VCUP, UMTRA, solid-waste and environmental-covenant registers, matched by location (40–100 m by inventory, grown by the site's spread up to 250 m), by a record at the same address (street type and unit ignored) or by name; "no match" means no record in those searched inventories, not an absence of contamination or of assessment. Under this rule 1,212 of the 3,487 sites (35%) match no record, but 1,008 of the 2,275 matched sites (44%) are matched only because a differently named record, most often a small-quantity hazardous-waste notifier, lies within the radius (or a mapped cleanup boundary lies within 40 m). Counting only a record with the same name or at the same address, or a cleanup boundary, 64% match no record; doubling every radius, 21%. An address match to a differently named record counts as the same entity, since the contamination belongs to the lot; about 490 sites are matched only by address, to a record whose name shares no informative word with any firm listed there, but most of them also have a differently named record within the radius, and counting the roughly 100 that do not as unmatched would raise the 35% to about 38%. The tracts that hold an unmatched site differ from other tracts in the same way under every rule in this range (higher EnviroScreen scores, more low-income residents and fewer high-school graduates).

**Designs.** Section 3 regresses each district measure on the log distance from the district centroid to the nearest hazardous firm of the decade, weighted by population with HC1 errors, census by census and pooled with census effects, and repeats each model with the log distance to downtown as a control, because both industry and the poorest housing were central. Segregation is measured on each census's own districts and, because the number and size of districts change, also on a fixed 500 m grid onto which each census is area-weighted. Section 4 follows Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder (2021): for each pair of adjacent HOLC grades, units within 400 m (districts) or 800 m (tracts) of both grades are compared across the boundary, with rail distance and 1929 industrial zoning as controls; and follows Xu (2022) in regressing the change in each tract outcome on the tract's dominant 1938 class (A reference; the ungraded Industrial and Commercial zones, four tracts each, dropped), with the starting value, 1940 nonwhite share and 1931–40 firms controlled; the A class has only four tracts and B six, so every class contrast rests on few reference tracts. Firm densities by grade are compared per directory edition and within each source, never across the change of source in 1957–65. Whether the D–C gap trended is tested within one unit type at a time (districts 1900–40; tracts 1940–70 and 1970–2020). Section 4 then tabulates, without models, sequences on fixed geographies: a 500 m grid over the area mapped in every census 1900–1940 (310 cells, each assigned its future 1938 class and its 1929 zoning) and the 142 study tracts observed in every census from 1960, with location quotients (a group's share in a set of places divided by its share in the whole area) and comparisons of places before and after a first hazardous firm arrived. Section 5 repeats the Section 3 design on tracts and estimates a hybrid within/between panel (Marlow, Frickel and Elliott 2020) for 1970–1990, the only censuses with active complete-edition firms, with tract-clustered errors and a between-tract spatial-error model; 2000–2020 are analysed as cross-sections around the 1983–92 sites, and a reverse model asks whether composition at one census predicts the change in a tract's number of hazardous establishments by the next. Section 6 tabulates current parcel use by series and by the decade of last listing. Standard errors are HC1 in the main tables; Section 4 reports the boundary and change estimates again with Conley (2 km) spatially robust errors, narrower and wider boundary bands, and a strict version of the zoning layer.

## 3. Who lived next to industry, 1880–1940

In every census from 1880 to 1940 the Denver districts with a smelter, foundry, tannery or other hazardous trade held more foreign-born and labouring residents, more boarders and lodgers and fewer owners than the rest of the city. The Black and Hispanic patterns are less uniform. Districts with a firm were more Black in 1880, 1900 and 1940 but not in 1910, 1920 or 1930, and more Hispanic in every census but 1910, markedly so from 1920. Some of what looks like proximity to industry is proximity to downtown, where both industry and the poorest housing stood. The cross-sections describe who lived where; they cannot by themselves say whether people followed industry or industry followed people.

**Units and sources.** Each census 1880–1940 is reconstructed at the enumeration district (17 EDs in 1880, 128 in 1900, 158 in 1910, 309 in 1920, 228 in 1930, 330 in 1940). Hazardous trades are the street-level listings of the business directory in the Frickel & Elliott hazardous sectors; a decade's firms are those listed in the ten years ending at the census (60, 21, 5, 103, 21 and 43 firms), and 12, 12, 4, 40, 17 and 27 districts contain one. The small number of firm districts in 1910 (4) and 1930 (17) makes those comparisons fragile.

Three readings follow, each narrower than a simple story of industry and race.

1. *Class and nativity.* The laborer, ownership, SEI and foreign-born gradients are present in nearly every census and survive the downtown control, although the ownership and lodging gradients shrink by half or more once centrality is held constant. This is the most secure pre-zoning pattern in the data: Denver's hazardous trades stood among the city's poorer, rented and immigrant housing well before 1925.
2. *Race and ethnicity.* The Black gradient is negative in all six censuses and significant in five, and it strengthens with the downtown control (−1.48 pooled). Black Denverites were a small population, about 2.4–2.8% of the city in every census, so the gradient rests on few districts and the with/without comparison flips with them (Black shares were lower in firm districts in 1910 and 1930). The Hispanic gradient, by contrast, is a downtown gradient: it appears from 1920, steepens to −2.40 in 1940, and falls to −0.10 to −0.17 and loses significance once distance to downtown is controlled. Before 1940 Hispanic Denver was concentrated in the central districts, which were also the industrial ones; the analysis cannot distinguish proximity to the plants from proximity to the centre for this group.
3. *Workers.* Without the downtown control the share of a district's workers employed in the hazardous industries does not vary with distance from the plants; with it, those workers lived closer to them (−0.77 per log m). The census gives industry of employment, not workplace, so this is consistent with, but does not prove, workers living near their plants once the pull of the centre is set aside. An earlier draft of this paper read the uncontrolled estimate as evidence against a workers'-housing account; the controlled estimate does not support that reading.

**Segregation.** The Black–nonblack dissimilarity index across districts rose from 0.41 in 1880 to 0.82 in 1940, but the districts became smaller and more numerous over the period (17 to 330), and dissimilarity rises mechanically as units shrink. ## 4. The pre-trend: before and after the map

The 1938 HOLC map of Denver recorded an industrial geography that was already forty years old. The land graded D had held more hazardous trades per square kilometre than C land since the first decade of the century, and the 1925 ordinance had zoned it industrial nine times as often as C land (15.9% against 1.7%). A Black gap at the D–C boundaries opened in the 1920s and 1930s, before the map, but it is imprecisely estimated. After the map, hazardous manufacturing continued to concentrate in D and ungraded industrial land, and the D tracts became poorer and lost value relative to A tracts; their Hispanic share rose more than the A tracts' after 1970, but not by a statistically clear margin. The analysis cannot isolate the contribution of the HOLC grading itself from zoning, FHA practice, rail and the post-war land market. The design follows Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder (2021) and Faber (2020) in asking whether the map *created* or *reinforced* the pattern, and Fishback et al. (2024) in treating the appraisers as describing more than deciding.

**Industry by grade, before and after 1938.** Of the 209 street-level hazardous trades listed before the map within the graded and ungraded HOLC areas, none stood in an A or B area; 12 stood in C (in 7 of 20 areas), 22 in D (in 5 of 16), and the remaining 175 in the zones the appraisers left ungraded as Industrial (27) or Commercial (148, mostly the downtown and Larimer Street fringe). The appraisers' remarks mention industry in 8 of the 16 D descriptions and 5 of the 20 C descriptions. Of the 780 hazardous-sector manufacturers of the complete 1965–1992 editions located in these areas, 47.4% were in D areas, which hold 26.0% of the land, and a further 23.7% in the ungraded Industrial areas (12.1% of the land).

Covenants were recorded in every grade (40 deeds in A areas, 26 in B, 144 in C and 216 in D; 440 of the 730 name Black residents, 27 also bar manufacturing). Within D, the 11 districts with a covenant held 1.1% Black residents in 1930 against 8.9% in the 51 without, consistent with covenants guarding white blocks within and beside the D areas.

**Before the map: 1903.** The 1903–04 Sanborn atlas (Library of Congress) shows how housing and industry sat together before any district was drawn. We read 152 sheets covering the north side from Lower Downtown to Elyria and Swansea, and Auraria, West Denver and the South Platte corridor, block by block, and placed 122 of them on today's streets. Of the 8,232 dwellings on those sheets, 22% stood on a block with a hazardous trade (a foundry, smelter, metal or machine works, chemical or gas works, tannery, packing house, brick kiln, rail shop or power plant), a further 29% were directly across a street from one, and 78% were within 150 m of one. Who lived there followed the same gradient: in the 1910 enumeration districts, owner households were 11% of households where dwellings shared a block with a hazardous trade, 17% across the street from one and 33% where none stood within 150 m. The 1925 ordinance did not separate this mix. Only 32% of the 1903 blocks with a hazardous trade fell in an Industrial A district on the 1929 map, and 46% fell in residence or business districts. The places persisted all the same: 92% of those blocks had a hazardous-sector manufacturer of 1965–92 within 150 m.

**The 1925 zoning ordinance.** Denver zoned thirteen years before the HOLC map. On the *Building Zone Map* as revised to January 1929, 28.4 km² of the city was zoned Industrial A or B and 3.6 km² Commercial. Of the D land on the map sheet, 15.9% was zoned industrial in 1929; of the C land 1.7%, B 0.3% and A 0.7%. The 1925 ordinance is the first map of the same geography, drawn around the industrial corridor that Section 3 shows was in place by 1900–1920.

**1929 to 1944.** The districts barely moved across the years of the HOLC survey. Between the January 1929 and April 1944 revisions no industrial district was added. About 0.48 km² of Industrial A (2% of it) was redrawn: as commercial districts near W 41st Avenue, as business blocks in River North (Brighton Boulevard and 36th Street) and by the rail yards near E 40th Avenue, and as apartment blocks (Residence D) in two small areas. On the land both sheets cover, the share of D land zoned industrial went from 16.0% to 15.8%, and of C land stayed at 1.7%. The zoning the appraisers saw in 1938 was in substance the zoning of 1929, and it was not extended into the areas they graded D by 1944.

**Zone changes, 1925–1956.** The two map sheets fix the districts at two dates; the ordinances that amended them record every change in between. We read all 558 zoning-related ordinances of 1923–1956 in the City's scanned archive, setting aside the Board of Adjustment's letters and owners' petitions filed with them, and found 288 changes of district in 196 ordinances, 38 of them newly annexed land zoned for the first time. Each was placed from the subdivision block it names (matched to today's parcel legal descriptions) or from the streets its boundary follows; 214 were placed, 119 of them by a named block or by street crossings that a named subdivision confirms. Rezoning to industry was rare: nine residential areas became industrial, two in 1928 and seven in 1955–1956, and every one of those that falls inside a graded area lay in a C or D area. What changed over time was the conversion of residential land to business. It was 13% of rezonings in 1925–1945, when most changes returned the commercial frontages drawn in 1925 to residence, 40% in 1946–1954, and 62% in 1955–1956 under the new code. By the 1938 grade of the area, 52% of rezonings in D areas (21 of 40) turned residential land over to business or industry, against 10% in A areas and 17% in B areas (Fisher's exact p = 0.002, D against A and B), and 90% of rezonings in A areas went the other way, to residence. Part of the gap is timing: 23 of the D areas' 40 rezonings came in 1955–1956, when such rezonings were common everywhere, and A areas had none after 1945. Compared within each period the odds ratio is 3.9 (exact Mantel–Haenszel p = 0.08), and among the most securely placed changes the gap points the same way but is not statistically clear (Fisher's p = 0.12). The ordinances therefore add a suggestive pattern to the map evidence: zoning did not extend industry into the D areas, but it let their residential land be given over to other uses more readily than in A and B areas.

**Interpretation.** The evidence answers the three questions as follows, each within the limits of the data. *Did the zoning or the map create the disparities?* No. The class and nativity gradient around hazardous industry is present from 1880; the trades that opened before 1940 opened where poorer, Black and immigrant households already lived; the Black concentration in the future D land had tripled before the map and was largely in place before the ordinance; and the industrially zoned land had been the city's most immigrant and labouring land since 1900. *What preceded the disparities?* The industrial corridor along the Platte and the rail yards, and the housing market that put the city's poorest, newest and Black residents near the centre and the tracks. The two later diverged: Black residents concentrated in residential blocks northeast of downtown, Mexican and Hispano residents in the industrial blocks. *Did the instruments or later siting accelerate the disparities?* The data show no acceleration at 1925 or 1938 that can be attributed to either instrument. Within the business directory hazardous siting after 1938 favoured D land within the range it had before (D/C 1.9, against 1.7–6.2), and within the manufacturers' directory the ratio moved from 13.8 to 18.2; Black concentration in D rose most in 1910–30, before the map; the D–C gaps that are significant after 1940 are Hispanic ones, and both the Hispanic and Black concentrations in D and industrial tracts peaked around 1960 and then declined. What the instruments did was record the geography, in an ordinance that zoned for industry the land industry already occupied and an appraisal that marked the same land as hazardous to lend in. Whether that recording slowed the decline of the concentrations, prevented investment, or kept industry in place cannot be identified from these data: the zoning and the appraisers' Industrial designation overlap too closely, the boundary gaps are imprecise, and there is no comparison city without the instruments.

**Land use and freeways, 1937–1997.** The directories place firms and the zoning maps record what was allowed; the U.S. Geological Survey's historical land-use maps of the northern Front Range record what the land was used for, interpreted by hand from aerial photographs of 1937, 1957, 1977 and 1997 at 1 m with a minimum mapping unit of about a hectare (Drummond et al. 2019). Grouped into eight categories and summarised on 10 m cells, they show that industrial land use was concentrated in D areas before the HOLC map: 5.3% of D land was in industrial use in 1937, against at most 0.5% of A, B or C land, and 9.0% by 1977. (Ibsen et al. 2025, using the same maps, report about a quarter of D land as industrial in 1937; recomputed cell by cell from the 1 m rasters on the Mapping Inequality D polygons, the area-weighted share is again 5.3%, so the difference lies in how the grades are summarised.) Zoning ran ahead of use. In 1937 two-thirds of the city's industrial land lay in the 1929 industrial districts, but only 19% of those districts was in industrial use; the share reached 38% by 1977, so most of the zoned land filled in after the HOLC map rather than before it. The maps also offer an independent check on the inventory, since they were drawn without reference to the directories: within 30 m, 49% of the 1965–92 sites on a current regulatory inventory stood on land the interpreters marked industrial in 1977, against 21% of the sites on no inventory, consistent with the parcel evidence (Section 6) that many of the latter were small shops on mixed blocks.

Freeways added a second, later industrial geography. Dated alignments from OpenHistoricalMap, checked against the NBER/FHWA interstate opening dates (Baum-Snow 2007; I-25 opened to interstate standard in Denver County in 1958–1965, I-70 in 1965–1968), put 69 km of freeway in the study area by 1970 (a divided freeway, mapped as two carriageways, counted once). Freeways built in 1950–1979 ran 0.37 km per km² through D land, against 0.09 in C, 0.01 in B and none in A, and 17% of D land lies within 250 m of one. A quarter of their length crossed the 1929 industrial districts, which were 12% of the mapped land, and two-thirds crossed residence and business districts. The 34 study tracts they cut through were already more Hispanic in 1960 (13% against 7.5% elsewhere) and lost more population afterwards (92% of their 1950 population in 1990, against 99%). To see what followed the openings rather than what preceded them, each of the 48 study tracts with a freeway opened within 250 m in 1950–1979 was lined up on its own opening year, and its population-weighted gap from tracts with no freeway within 1 km was traced from the last census before the opening; most of these openings fell in 1951–1968. Against inner tracts at a similar distance from downtown (within 5.8 km, 34 tracts), the freeway tracts' white share went from 8 points above theirs at the last census before opening to 7 points below four decades later, and the owner-occupancy gap narrowed from 16 to 9 points. The Hispanic share can be followed from before the opening only for the 13 tracts whose freeway opened in the 1960s, since the census first counted Hispanic origin in 1960: their gap went from 1 point at the last census before opening to 4, 6 and 9 points over the next three decades and 18 points after four. The 35 tracts whose freeway opened in the 1950s have no pre-opening Hispanic count; they were already 11 points more Hispanic than the inner comparison tracts in 1960 and 28 points by 2000 (12 and 21 points against all tracts with no nearby freeway). Poverty, first measured in 1970, has no pre-opening value for any of these tracts and is not traced. Population fell in both groups, and less in the freeway tracts (to 85% of the pre-opening count after four decades, against 70%). Against all tracts with no nearby freeway the direction is the same (Hispanic gap from 2 to 12 points for the 1960s cohort, white from 5 to −9). This is a comparison of trends, not a causal estimate: the routes were chosen through places already changing, and the owner-occupancy gap was already shrinking in the decades before opening. What it does show is that the neighbourhoods beside the new freeways became steadily more Hispanic and less white than comparable inner neighbourhoods, while keeping more of their residents.

**Urban renewal.** Federal urban renewal, run in Denver from 1958 by the Denver Urban Renewal Authority, cleared a different set of places. Renewing Inequality (Nelson, Winling et al.) maps six Denver projects from the federal Urban Renewal Project Characteristics reports. Two of the authority's first four projects were industrial in plan: 23.6 of Blake Street's 45.7 acres and 4.3 of Jerome Park's 6.3 acres were to be reused for industry, and the two areas held 3 and 4 pre-1960 hazardous trades and 13 and 25 of the 1965–92 hazardous-sector manufacturers. The reports list 189 families in the Blake Street area (181 white, 8 non-white) and 69 white families in Jerome Park; at Whittier School, in the Black neighbourhood northeast of downtown, 128 of the 132 families were non-white, and the land went mostly to new housing. Skyline, approved by voters in 1967, cleared 120 acres of downtown where about 1,600 people lived (Barnhouse, quoted in Denverite 2017), and its area had held 43 of the pre-1960 hazardous trades. Auraria, heavily Hispanic, flooded in 1965 and approved as a college campus by a bond vote in 1969, is not mapped in the federal data. Area-weighted census figures, which borrow the composition of the surrounding tracts and so illustrate rather than test, show the project areas changing as the freeway tracts did: the Jerome Park area went from 56% Hispanic in 1960 to 79% in 1970 while its estimated population halved (850 to 421), and the Blake Street area lost two-fifths of its residents between 1960 and 1980 (598 to 362).

## 6. What became of the sites

Of the 1,112 hazardous-sector manufacturing sites listed in Denver between 1965 and 1992, 50% are still industrial parcels today, 26% are commercial, 16% residential and 5% vacant, and 54% are still zoned industrial. Of the 395 pre-1960 hazardous trades, 59% are now commercial, 20% residential and 10% industrial, and only 15% remain in an industrial zone. Only 13% of the later sites, but 44% of the earlier ones, carry a building newer than their last directory listing. 184 of the later sites are residential parcels holding 5,637 dwelling units; 97 of those 184 (53%) match no record in any inventory searched, cleanup or otherwise (146 have no cleanup record), which does not establish that they were never assessed or remediated.

**Method.** Every geocoded site in the City and County of Denver (1,112 from the 1965–1992 manufacturers' directory in the Frickel & Elliott hazardous sectors, and 395 street-level hazardous trades of the 1891–1956 business directories; all of the former and all but one of the latter have a parcel within 40 m) is matched to the assessor parcel within 40 m whose situs address matches the directory address (else the same street and side, else the same street, else the nearest parcel; median distance 4 m). A match identifies the parcel at the geocoded address today, not necessarily the historical lot: street-centreline geocodes, consolidated or subdivided parcels and multi-building sites all need checking against the Sanborn sheets before an individual site is cited. The parcel carries the assessor's property class, the current zone district, the dwelling-unit count and the year the present building was built. "Redeveloped" means the building post-dates the site's last directory year. This is the land-use-conversion question Frickel & Elliott (2008) posed for a sample of Portland and Philadelphia sites, answered here for the whole Denver inventory.

Two results matter for the argument of Sections 4 and 5. First, the industrial corridor of the 1960s–90s is largely still there: half the sites are industrial parcels and more than half (54%) are still in an industrial zone district, so the industrial land beside the Hispanic neighbourhoods of 1970–2000 has not been removed, only thinned at its edges. Second, the pre-1960 trades — the smelters, foundries, tanneries and rendering works of the Platte bottoms and the downtown fringe — have almost all turned over: nearly six in ten are commercial, one in five residential, and 44% have been rebuilt. These resemble the "relict" sites of Elliott & Frickel (2013): their last listings fall decades before the regulatory inventories searched here began, which is consistent with, though does not by itself explain, their absence from those inventories.

**Residential sites are mostly not conversions.** For 119 of the 184 residential sites of 1965–92 the present building was built in or before the site's last listing year (47 were built later; 18 have no date). Most of these are therefore small firms that operated in or beside houses and apartment buildings, or geocodes that fell on a neighbouring dwelling, rather than factories later replaced by housing. Of the 97 residential sites on no inventory, 76 are of this kind. Of the 78 pre-1960 trades now residential, 36 carry a building newer than the listing. The 111 later sites now in residential (97), civic (8) or park (6) use that match no inventory record, and the 78 pre-1960 trade sites now residential, are the cases for archival follow-up: the Sanborn sheets show what stood on the lot and what it stored, and the Polk reverse directories give the sequence of occupants. The 1890 Sanborn check in Section 2 is the first step of that work.

The comprehensive ordinance of 1955 enlarged the industrial districts rather than trimming them. Of the added land, 2.6 km² lay in areas graded D in 1938, while C areas lost nearly all of their small industrial share (1.8% to 0.1%). Industrial zoning thus reached its greatest extent, and its greatest concentration in D areas, at mid-century, when the ordinances also record most of the conversions of residential land to business and industry (above).

The contraction came late. Of the 28.4 km² zoned industrial in 1929, 52% is still zoned industrial, 11% is industrial mixed use, 15% commercial or mixed use, 10% residential and 13% open space, civic land or planned unit developments. For the 13.7 km² no longer zoned industrial, the ordinance that last zoned the land passed before 2000 for under 3% of it, in 2000–09 for 12%, in the 2010 citywide remapping for 53%, and after 2010 for 33%. Because the city layer keeps only the latest ordinance, earlier rezonings are hidden, and land recoded in 2010 may have left industrial zoning at any time after 1944; the date is therefore an upper bound. ## 7. Historical hazardous sites and mortality

The last question is whether the historical sites matter for health now. Natural-cause and all-cause deaths in 2015–2019 from Colorado death records were assigned to the 2010 census block of residence (95% of deaths matched a 2010 block) and modelled for every Colorado block with at least 100 person-years (59,293 blocks in 1,241 tracts; 132,051 natural-cause deaths) and separately for the Denver study area (10,395 blocks in 184 tracts; 24,399 natural-cause deaths). Negative binomial models with county fixed effects, an offset for person-years and standard errors clustered by tract control for the block's age structure (the share aged 65 and over and its square, and the share under 5, from the 2010 census), population density and mean PM2.5 in 2015–2019; for the tract's poverty, Hispanic, Black and owner-occupied shares in 2020; and for the EnviroScreen low-income, education, linguistic-isolation and hazardous-waste and Superfund proximity indicators. Exposure is a Gaussian kernel density (500 m bandwidth, 2 km cutoff) around each block, standardised, for five sets of points: the 1965–92 hazardous-sector sites that appear in a current regulatory inventory ("known"), those that appear in none ("hidden"), the street-level pre-1960 hazardous trades, current TRI and cleanup sites, and, as a placebo, manufacturers outside the hazardous sectors. The death records are restricted and remain with the author; only model estimates are reported.

Blocks near the known historical sites have about 4% higher mortality per standard deviation of exposure statewide, after the controls and after the current TRI and cleanup sites, with an interval that just reaches 1 for natural causes (1.036, 0.997–1.076) and excludes it for all causes (1.041, 1.004–1.080), and about 6% higher in the Denver study area, where the interval is wide (0.973–1.163). Estimated without the hidden sites in the model, the known-site ratio is 1.050 (1.015–1.087) statewide and 1.075 (1.007–1.149) in Denver. The association shrinks with the placebo in the model (1.032, 0.992–1.073). Blocks near the hidden sites show a smaller association that is not statistically clear (1.026, 0.996–1.056), and the high-confidence subset shows none (1.000, 0.971–1.029). Two readings fit these results. The known sites were the larger and longer-lived operations, which is why they entered the inventories; about half of them stood on land the aerial-photograph interpreters marked industrial in 1977, against under a quarter of the hidden sites (Section 5), most of which were small shops on mixed blocks. And part of the known-site association may reflect industrial neighbourhoods as such rather than the hazardous sectors: the placebo alone is of the same size as the known sites (1.038, 1.003–1.074), and with both in the model neither is statistically clear (placebo 1.019, 0.982–1.056; known sites 1.032, 0.992–1.073). The inverse association of the pre-1960 trades most likely reflects what else characterises the old urban core in which they stood, including the redevelopment and turnover described in Section 6, rather than a protective effect. These are associations across places in one period: residence at death is not lifetime exposure, a kernel density ignores what each site handled and for how long, and the controls cannot rule out residual confounding.

**Before and after the sites were rebuilt.** If the old works mattered, mortality nearby should fall once a site is rebuilt. The same death records cover 2000–2019, so a block-year panel for the Denver study area (about 200,000 block-years) was built with each block's population that year. A site counts as rebuilt from the year its parcel's present structure was built, when that is after its last directory listing (Section 6); in 2000–2019 that happened to 20 hidden sites, 51 known sites and 52 pre-1960 trades. Poisson models with block and year fixed effects, a population offset and yearly PM2.5 compare each block with itself as nearby sites went from standing to rebuilt. Per standard deviation of nearby sites still standing, natural-cause mortality was no different: 1.03 (0.84–1.27) for hidden sites, 1.00 (0.68–1.47) for known sites and 1.01 (0.86–1.20) for pre-1960 trades. Around the first rebuild within 400 m (924 blocks, against 3,630 blocks near sites that stood throughout), mortality in every year before and after the rebuild was indistinguishable from the year before it (1.05, 0.92–1.21, four or more years before; 1.06, 0.92–1.21, two years before; 1.05, 0.90–1.22, four or more years after). The new building therefore left no trace in the mortality of the blocks around it; rebuilding also brings younger residents, whose age the offset does not capture, so a small effect of the site itself could be hidden.

**Where people lived in 1940 and how long they lived.** The 2015–2019 association is with residence at death. CenSoc links the 1940 complete-count census to Social Security death records (Breen, Osborne and Goldstein 2023): the CenSoc-DMF file covers men dying in 1975–2020 and CenSoc-Numident men and women dying in 1988–2005. Because the Brown address files carry the same person identifier as the census, each linked Denver resident of 1940 could be placed at their 1940 street address: 9,769 men in the DMF file and 14,936 people in the Numident file, of whom 8,390 and 13,376 lived in households (not institutions) and entered the models. At that address were measured the 1929 zoning district, the share of land within 250 m in industrial use in 1937 (Section 5), the hazardous trades listed in the directories up to 1940, the HOLC grade, and the 1965–92 sites. ## 8. Discussion and conclusion

The paper set out to ask whether Denver's zoning and redlining created the city's environmental inequality, what preceded it, and whether the instruments or later industrial siting accelerated it. On the first question the answer is no. The class and nativity ordering of the population around hazardous industry — labouring, immigrant, lodging and renting households near it, owners and higher-status workers away from it — is present from the earliest mapped census, survives a control for distance to downtown, and was in place decades before the city zoned, before deeds carried racial covenants and before the HOLC appraisers arrived. The hazardous trades that opened before 1940 opened where such households already lived.

On the second question, what preceded the disparities was an industrial corridor and a housing market, and the two produced different geographies for different groups. Black Denverites, a small population, lived near the centre and the industry in 1880–1910, and between 1910 and 1930 became concentrated in residential blocks northeast of downtown that the appraisers later graded D; segregation on fixed geography doubled between 1880 and 1940, most steeply in 1920–30. The land the 1925 ordinance zoned industrial was the city's most immigrant and labouring land from 1900, lost its Black residents, and became Mexican and Hispano by 1920–30. The HOLC D grade drew both into one category, which is why a D–C Black gap and a D–C Hispanic gap both appear and why neither, read alone, describes the industrial geography.

On the third question, the data show no acceleration at 1925 or 1938 that can be attributed to either instrument. Within the business directory hazardous siting after 1938 favoured D land within the range it had before (D/C 1.9, against 1.7–6.2), and within the manufacturers' directory the ratio moved from 13.8 to 18.2; the Black concentration in D rose most before the map; and both the Black and the Hispanic concentrations in D and industrial tracts peaked around 1960 and have declined since. After 1950 the group most concentrated beside industry was Hispanic on every geography and specification tested, and D tracts became poorer and lost value relative to A tracts, while their Hispanic share rose more than the A tracts' after 1970 by a margin that is not statistically clear. That association is a property of places rather than of changes within them: tracts did not change composition when their industry changed, composition also predicts where the number of hazardous firms grew, and the manufacturers that arrived after 1965 went to tracts that were no poorer than those that never had one and were followed by much faster Hispanic growth. The corridor is still largely industrial in the parcel record.

What remains unidentified is the effect of any one instrument. The grading, the zoning, FHA practice, the railways and the post-war land market pointed at the same land; a boundary design with fifteen tracts a side cannot pull them apart; and the pre-map D–C Black gap is consistent in sign but not significant once spatial dependence is allowed for.

The reading we draw is closer to "recording" than to either "creation" or "reinforcement" in the terms of Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder (2021) and Fishback et al. (2024): reinforcement may have occurred, but these data cannot show it. For Denver the question of whether the HOLC map caused the later divergence matters less than the observation that the instruments the city and the federal government produced between 1925 and 1938 recorded a corridor that was already a generation or two old, and that the corridor then held successive disadvantaged groups. That is the "churning" that Frickel and Elliott describe from the industrial side, seen from the residential side: the industrial landscape persists, and the people pass through it.

Two implications follow for environmental-justice research. The first is about measurement. A study that begins with the regulatory inventories of the 1980s begins after Denver's pre-1960 hazardous trades had closed and their sites had been converted; it observes a Hispanic–industry association whose geography was laid down earlier. The directory series and the full-count census make the earlier period measurable, and the methods here are not specific to Denver. The checks needed along the way are part of the method: in this paper a single study area, fixed-geography checks, a downtown control, within-source density comparisons, a direction check on the geocodes and a split of the D grade by zoning each changed a result that an earlier draft had stated more strongly. The second implication is about the relict sites. About one in six of the 1965–92 hazardous sites and one in five of the pre-1960 trades now stand under housing; most of the later ones were small firms in residential buildings rather than conversions, and 97 carry no record in any inventory this project searched. Whether they are contaminated is unknown; they are also largely invisible to the inventories that would find out.

The limitations are set out where they arise and are restated here. The district reconstruction places 72% of the 1900 addresses (60% of the population once stray geocodes are dropped) at street level and the rest by district; Hispanic origin before 1960 is inferred, not self-reported. The directory inventory has twelve partial editions set aside and a change of source between 1956 and 1965, so firm densities are compared only within each source. The 1929 zoning layer cannot fully separate apartment from industrial hatching and does not cover the whole graded area. The tract analysis interpolates every census before 1990 by area. The pre-map Black gap at the D–C boundary is consistent but imprecise. The post-2000 exposure is to historically listed sites. The descriptive sequences use area weighting from districts to grid cells, which assumes people are spread evenly within a district. The archival check covers twenty addresses on one Sanborn edition. The next pass on this paper is the reverse-directory and later Sanborn checks of the residential sites, not further interpretation.
